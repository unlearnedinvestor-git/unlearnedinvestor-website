<p>The entire world is going absolutely crazy over the rumor of a SpaceX Initial Public Offering (IPO). Financial headlines are treating it like the biggest event in market history. Mainstream media outlets are running constant calculations on how this valuation will officially make Elon Musk the world&#8217;s first trillionaire. The hype is everywhere, and the temptation to get a piece of it is incredibly strong.</p><p>But as retail investors, we have to separate the loud, emotional media noise from the cold, data-driven fundamental signals.</p><h2>The Numbers Behind the Buzz</h2><p>Because SpaceX is a private company, it has zero legal obligation to share its financial performance with the public. What we know comes from unverified media leaks and financial reports from outlets like Bloomberg.</p><p>According to <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91549280/spacexs-1-75-trillion-ipo-pitch-relies-on-a-lot-of-ai-faith">these leaks</a>, SpaceX isn&#8217;t just a rocket business. It is a conglomerate split into three distinct arms, with projected numbers for the current cycle:</p><ol><li><p><strong>Space Transport (Rockets):</strong> Rumored revenue is <strong>$4.1 billion</strong>, operating with a loss of roughly <strong>$657 million</strong>.</p></li><li><p><strong>Starlink (Satellite Internet):</strong> The real cash engine. It pulled in an estimated <strong>$11.4 billion</strong> in revenue, generating a <strong>$4.4 billion</strong> profit. This is currently the only profitable side of the company.</p></li><li><p><strong>AI &amp; Infrastructure (xAI/Grok Data Centers):</strong> Reports point to <strong>$3.2 billion</strong> in revenue, but with staggering infrastructure losses of <strong>$6.4 billion</strong>.</p></li></ol><p>If we total these numbers up, SpaceX is pulling in an estimated <strong>$18.7 billion</strong> in combined revenue.</p><h2>The Catch: Unaudited Private Data</h2><p>Before your gut convinces you to hunt down a way to buy into this hype, your brain needs to understand where this data comes from.</p><p>None of these numbers are audited. None of them are verified by independent financial regulators. In the private markets, a company can present its numbers exactly how it wants to frame them. It is under no public standard of accountability. Basing your investment thesis on unverified numbers from a news article isn&#8217;t a calculated risk&#8212;it is a pure gamble.</p><h2>What an IPO Actually Means</h2><p>An IPO&#8212;or Initial Public Offering&#8212;is simply the moment a private company opens its doors to let the general public buy its shares for the first time.</p><p>The financial industry markets an IPO as a special gift to the everyday investor, giving you a chance to buy in on the ground floor. In reality, an IPO is an exit strategy for early-stage venture capitalists, institutional funds, and insiders. They have had their money locked up in a private club for a decade. The IPO is their payday. It is their prime opportunity to sell their private stakes to the public at the absolute highest price they can get.</p><p>Because existing investors want to lock in peak profits, a company going public has every incentive to inflate its narrative, show aggressive projections, and achieve a sky-high valuation multiple.</p><h2>Reality Check: The Data Table</h2><p>To understand how inflated an IPO price can be, we need to look at <strong>Earnings Per Share (EPS)</strong> and <strong>Price-to-Sales (P/S)</strong> metrics. Let&#8217;s compare the rumored, unaudited numbers of SpaceX against actual, transparent public companies with audited financials as of today (May 28, 2026):</p><div id="datawrapper-iframe" class="datawrapper-wrap outer" data-attrs="{&quot;url&quot;:&quot;https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/P237y/1/&quot;,&quot;thumbnail_url&quot;:&quot;https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/d4c61c3f-da85-4043-a630-9554cacccacf_1220x942.png&quot;,&quot;thumbnail_url_full&quot;:&quot;https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f05e4c5f-64c3-455b-b2d3-e59e2c0222d5_1220x1012.png&quot;,&quot;height&quot;:516,&quot;title&quot;:&quot;Companies and their P/S Ratio against SpaceX&quot;,&quot;description&quot;:&quot;&quot;,&quot;belowTheFold&quot;:true}" data-component-name="DatawrapperToDOM"><iframe id="iframe-datawrapper" class="datawrapper-iframe" src="https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/P237y/1/" width="730" height="516" frameborder="0" scrolling="no" loading="lazy"></iframe></div><p>When you look at public giants like Alphabet and Apple, the math is clear. Alphabet generated an actual, audited profit of $5.11 per share for the quarter, making its $390.13 stock price backed by cold hard cash. Apple cleared an all-time record profit of $2.84 per share, making its $312.51 stock price highly reliable.</p><p>Now look at SpaceX. The rumors suggest the company wants a massive <strong>$1.75trillion</strong> valuation. At $18.7 billion in estimated revenue, you are being asked to pay a Price-to-Sales multiple of <strong>93.5x</strong>.</p><p>You would be paying the exact same premium for unverified, unaudited revenue from a loss-making rocket and AI business as you would pay for the highly stable, audited, multi-billion-dollar profit streams of Alphabet.</p><h2>Why You Should Wait and Watch</h2><p>Even if SpaceX is a great business, buying a stock on the day of its IPO is a massive gamble.</p><p>The smartest move you can make as an investor is to practice delayed gratification. You do not need to be first.</p><p>Once a company goes public, the legal game changes entirely. It is forced by law to release audited financial results every three months. The corporate walls become completely transparent.</p><ol><li><p><strong>Ignore the Initial Adrenaline:</strong> Let the day-one traders flip the stock. Let the early venture capitalists cash out their shares.</p></li><li><p><strong>Wait for Equilibrium:</strong> Within six to twelve months, the media hype always dies down. The stock price and the underlying corporate earnings will naturally find their equilibrium in the open market.</p></li><li><p><strong>Look at the Public Records:</strong> Wait until the company has published two or three quarters of audited, official numbers. Look at the real profit margins, the real debt metrics, and the real cash flows.</p></li></ol><p>An IPO is a single marketing event designed to maximize value for sellers, not buyers. Your real edge as a retail investor isn&#8217;t speed&#8212;it is patience. Wait for the noise to clear, look for the actual signal in the verified math, and only deploy your capital when it shifts from a blind gamble into a calculated risk.</p><div class="callout-block" data-callout="true"><p>DISCLAIMER: I am not a financial advisor. This is for educational purposes only. Always do your own research and speak with a certified financial professional before making investment decisions.</p></div></div></div></div><p></p>
